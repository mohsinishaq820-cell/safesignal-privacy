Spot a Phishing Email in 10 Seconds

Phishing emails are the single most common way people get hacked. Not sophisticated malware, not zero-day exploits — just a fake email that looks real enough to click. The good news: once you know what to look for, most phishing attempts fall apart in seconds.

Here are the five signs that give them away, every time.

1. The sender address doesn't match

The display name might say "Bank of America," but the actual email address is something like support@bankofamerica-secure-alerts.com or a string of random letters. Always check the real address, not just the name shown.

How to check: On desktop, hover over the sender's name without clicking. On mobile, tap the name once to expand the full address.

2. It creates urgency or fear

"Your account will be suspended in 24 hours." "Unusual sign-in detected — verify now." Real companies rarely demand instant action. Scammers use urgency to stop you from thinking clearly.

Rule of thumb: the more urgent the email sounds, the more suspicious you should be.

3. The link doesn't go where it says

The button might say "Verify Your Account," but the actual destination is a completely different, unfamiliar website.

How to check: Hover over the link (desktop) or long-press it (mobile) to preview the real URL before tapping.

4. Generic greetings and odd phrasing

"Dear Customer" instead of your name. Awkward grammar. Slightly-off formatting. Real companies you have accounts with usually know your name and have consistent email templates.

5. It asks for information no legitimate company needs by email

Passwords, full card numbers, Social Security numbers, one-time login codes — no legitimate bank, government agency, or service will ever ask for these directly over email.

What to do if you're not sure

  • Don't click any links or download any attachments.
  • Go directly to the company's website by typing the URL yourself, or use their official app.
  • If it claims to be from your bank, call the number on the back of your card — not any number in the email.
  • Report it — most email providers have a "Report Phishing" option.

What if you already clicked?

  1. Change the password for that account immediately, from a different device if possible.
  2. If you reuse that password elsewhere, change it everywhere.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication if it isn't already on.
  4. Watch your bank and card statements for the next few weeks.

Phishing works because it's designed to be convincing under pressure. The moment you slow down and check these five things, most of them stop working entirely.
