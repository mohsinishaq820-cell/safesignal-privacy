Do You Actually Need a VPN?

VPN ads are everywhere, often promising to make you "invisible" online. The reality is more specific: a VPN is genuinely useful in some situations and pointless in others. Here's how to tell which applies to you.

What a VPN actually does

A VPN encrypts your internet traffic and routes it through a server run by the VPN provider, hiding your traffic from your internet provider and anyone else on the same network. It does not make you anonymous — the VPN provider itself can usually still see your activity.

When a VPN genuinely helps

  • Public Wi-Fi — coffee shops, airports, hotels. These networks are the easiest place for someone to intercept your traffic.
  • Avoiding ISP tracking — some internet providers log and sell browsing data. A VPN prevents your ISP specifically from seeing what sites you visit.
  • Accessing content while traveling — useful for reaching home-region services abroad.

When it doesn't help much

  • It won't stop a website you're logged into (like Google or Facebook) from tracking you — they know it's you regardless of your IP address.
  • It won't protect you from phishing, malware, or a weak password.
  • On a trusted home network, the day-to-day benefit is small.

Free vs. paid

Free VPNPaid VPN
Often logs and sells your data to stay profitableReputable providers have independently audited no-log policies
Slower, limited dataFaster, unlimited data
Fewer server locationsMore locations, more reliable connections
Bottom line: if a VPN is free, you're often paying with your data instead of money — the opposite of what you're trying to protect.

If you regularly use public Wi-Fi or want your ISP out of your browsing history, a reputable paid VPN is worth it. If neither applies, your time is better spent on a password manager and two-factor authentication.
