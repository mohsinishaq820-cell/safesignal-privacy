Your Data Was Breached — Now What

A "your data may have been exposed" notification is unsettling, but panic doesn't help — a clear checklist does. Here's exactly what to do in the first hour, and in the weeks after.

First hour

  1. Change the password for the breached account immediately, from a device you trust.
  2. Change it everywhere else too if you reused that password on other sites — this is the single most important step.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication on the breached account and any related accounts if it isn't already active.
  4. Check what data was actually exposed. The company's breach notice usually specifies — passwords, card numbers, SSNs, or just email addresses each carry different risks.

If financial information was exposed

  • Contact your bank or card issuer to monitor for suspicious activity, or request a new card number.
  • Consider a free fraud alert or credit freeze with the major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) — this stops new accounts being opened in your name.

If your Social Security number was exposed

This is the most serious case. A credit freeze is strongly recommended, and it's worth monitoring your credit report for several months for any accounts you didn't open.

The weeks after

  • Watch for phishing emails referencing the breach — scammers often follow up pretending to "help."
  • Check your accounts periodically for the next few months, not just the first week.
  • Use a breach-notification service (many are free) to get alerted if your email shows up in future breaches.

Breaches are common enough that they're no longer a matter of if, only when. Having this checklist ready means the next one costs you twenty minutes instead of weeks of cleanup.
