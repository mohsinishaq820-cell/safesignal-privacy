Beginner Guide

Turn On Two-Factor Authentication

If there's one single change that does the most to protect an account, it's this one. Two-factor authentication (2FA) means that even if someone steals your password, they still can't get in without a second piece of proof that it's really you.

How it works

After entering your password, you're asked for a second code — usually from your phone. This can come from a text message, an authenticator app, or a physical security key. Without that second code, your password alone is useless to an attacker.

Which method should you use?

  • Authenticator app (recommended) — apps like Google Authenticator or Authy generate a rotating code on your phone, even offline. More secure than text messages.
  • Text message (SMS) — better than nothing, but vulnerable to a technique called SIM-swapping where attackers hijack your phone number. Fine for lower-risk accounts.
  • Security key — a physical USB or NFC device. The strongest option, mainly worth it for your most critical accounts (primary email, banking).
Priority order: security key > authenticator app > SMS > nothing. Any of these beats having no 2FA at all.

Where to turn it on first

  1. Your primary email — it's usually the recovery method for everything else, making it the highest-value target.
  2. Banking and financial apps.
  3. Social media accounts — frequently targeted for scams run through hijacked accounts.
  4. Password manager — protects everything stored inside it.

Save your backup codes

When you enable 2FA, most services give you one-time backup codes for if you lose your phone. Save these somewhere safe — printed out or in your password manager — not as a screenshot on the same phone.

Setting this up takes about two minutes per account. It's the highest return on time you'll find anywhere in this guide series.
